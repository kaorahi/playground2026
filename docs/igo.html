Help

Rules

  • Stones of the same color connect horizontally and vertically. Their edges facing empty points are colored. Block every colored edge to remove the whole group.
  • Play until you have no more moves you want to make. The player with more stones on the board wins. White gets 7.5 extra points.

Controls

  • Two consecutive passes end the game.
  • Use Auto when you want the computer to fill in the remaining moves.
  • In Review, tap or drag the yellow message bar below the board to move through moves. Auto-finished moves have a different tint.
  • In Review, hold ＜ or ＞ to move continuously.
  • Use ← or ↑ to go back one move. In Review, use → or ↓ to advance one move, and Home/End to jump to the beginning/end. Backspace undoes the last move.
  • In Edit mode, you can also right-click to remove stones.

About the bot

Level overview

  • Level 1: Randomly plays next to existing stones.
  • Level 2: Captures stones when possible, plays to escape atari, and otherwise follows Level 1.
  • Level 3: Mostly follows Level 2, occasionally Level 5.
  • Level 4: Mostly follows Level 5, occasionally Level 2.
  • Level 5: Looks one move ahead. Evaluates positions by stone counts, total liberties, and other factors.
  • Level 6: Looks three moves ahead. Adds average liberties per group to the evaluation.
  • Level 7: Looks five moves ahead. Gives more weight to average liberties in the evaluation.

See below for comparisons between levels and algorithm details.

Bot match results and Elo ratings

For each board size, Elo is estimated by maximum likelihood using the Bradley–Terry model. Players alternate black and white within each pairing. Games stopped at 12 × N² moves without two consecutive passes are classified as unfinished (excluded from calculations).

Shared definitions and rules

A group is a set of connected stones of one color; its liberties are the distinct adjacent empty intersections. An “eligible move” is a move on an empty point that does not violate simple ko and leaves the played stone on the board. To apply a move, first remove neighboring enemy groups with no liberties, then remove the new friendly group if it has no liberties. Simple ko forbids restoring the board from just before the preceding move; a pass clears this comparison. The bots choose eligible moves or pass.

Let N be the board size, with (x,y) = (0,0) at the upper left, and define the point index i = y × N + x. Two consecutive passes end the game; compare the number of black stones with the number of white stones plus 7.5.

An “eye-filling move” is an eligible move that captures no enemy stones and whose existing orthogonal neighbors are all friendly stones (including a move connecting a false eye). However, if the resulting friendly group has at least two liberties and an adjacent friendly group had just one liberty before the move (that is, a connection through a false eye rescuing a group in atari), the move is not classified as eye-filling. This definition is used both to filter moves for search and to decide whether ordinary play is finished.

A “tactical move” is an eligible move that captures at least one enemy stone or plays at the sole liberty of a friendly group that had one liberty before the move; the latter need not actually save that group. All other eligible moves are “non-tactical moves”. Tactical moves remain in the search move set regardless of their preliminary ranking.

Levels 1–4

Level 1: From all eligible moves, form a group of moves adjacent to an enemy stone and a group adjacent to a friendly stone. Randomly choose which group to prefer, each with probability 1/2. If that group is empty, use the other; if both are empty, use all eligible moves as the choice set. Choose uniformly at random from the choice set. Pass if there are no eligible moves.

Level 2: If any eligible move captures an enemy stone, choose uniformly at random from such moves. Otherwise, choose uniformly from eligible moves at the sole liberty of a friendly group that currently has one liberty (the move need not remove atari). If neither is available, use the Level 1 method.

Level 3: On each turn independently, choose the Level 2 method with probability 72%, or the Level 5 method with probability 28%.

Level 4: On each turn independently, choose the Level 2 method with probability 42%, or the Level 5 method with probability 58%.

Levels 5–7

Levels 5–7 differ in search depth d (counting the bot’s next move) and the average-liberty weight w in the position evaluation: Level 5 uses d = 1 and w = 0; Level 6 uses d = 3 and w = 5; Level 7 uses d = 5 and w = 10. At every level, the bot first constructs the ordered search move list M, obtains search scores S from position evaluations V at search leaves, and selects a move at random with probabilities determined by S (here V is evaluated from the viewpoint of the player who started the search). These quantities and the move lists are defined below.

Level 5: For each move m in M, set its search score S₁(m) to the position evaluation V immediately after playing m.

Level 6: For each move m in M, search the opponent's reply and the bot's next move. Use alpha–beta minimax to determine the search score S₃(m) for m from the position evaluations V at the search leaves.

Level 7: For each move m in M, calculate the three-ply search score S₃(m) as in Level 6. Let H be the ordered list of the three moves with highest S₃ (breaking ties by their original order in M). For each move m in H, search again five plies deep (with m as the first move) to obtain the search score S₅(m).

Position evaluation V

The position evaluation V is the sum of the stone score A, the liberty score D, and the average-liberty score G.

For each group on board P (with n stones and ℓ liberties), its stone score is αn (α = 65 if ℓ = 1, α = 90 if ℓ = 2, and α = 100 otherwise). Its liberty score is 2min(ℓ,6). The totals across all groups on P give A and D, counting groups belonging to the evaluating player positively and opposing groups negatively.

The average-liberty score G is the difference between the average liberties of the evaluating player’s groups and opposing groups, multiplied by weight w. To discourage unnatural moves, this app actually averages min(liberties,5) for each group. The average is 0 if there are no groups of that color.

For a position where two consecutive passes have ended the game, the position evaluation V is instead 100 times the difference between the evaluating player’s stone count and the opponent’s stone count.

Search move list M

The search does not examine every eligible move. At each position it builds the search move list M using the preliminary score Q and examines only the moves in M.

At each position, let U be the set of all eligible moves except eye-filling moves. For each move m in U, define the preliminary score Q by the following formula. Q = V + 2k − 0.12b Here V is the position evaluation after the move (from the viewpoint of the player making the move), k is the number of stones captured by that move, and b is its Manhattan distance from the board center.

Based on the preliminary score Q, define the search move list M as follows. Order the moves in U from highest to lowest Q, breaking ties by ascending point index i. Retain the top 12 moves and all tactical moves in M, preserving their Q order. However, in Level 7’s deeper search, from the second move onward, read “top 12 moves” as “top 2 moves” at each position. Append pass at the end of M, except for the 9×9 opening exception described below.

Move selection

The final candidate list C is the search move list M for Levels 5–6 and the deeper-search candidate list H for Level 7. The search score S used is S₁, S₃, or S₅, respectively. Let S_max be the highest search score in C. For each move m in C, retain it only if S_max − S(m) is at most the threshold (4 on 5×5, 2 on 7×7 and 9×9).

For each remaining move m, define the sampling weight W(m) by the following formula and select a move at random with probability proportional to W: W(m) = exp((S(m) − S_max − δ(m))/T) Here T is the temperature and δ(m) is the 9×9 opening adjustment described in the next section.

The temperature T decreases linearly from the first through the twelfth move and then stays constant. The initial and final temperatures are 2 → 0.5 on 5×5, 1 → 0.25 on 7×7, and 0.8 → 0.25 on 9×9.

Note: Here the move count means the number of recorded moves, including passes. When switching from Free mode to Play mode, only the most recent contiguous history of legal alternating moves is counted.

9×9 opening exceptions

To discourage unnatural moves, the following exceptions apply only in the opening on 9×9. They apply when the bot is about to play one of the first five moves and at most four stones are on the board. At the current root position, omit pass from M and exclude non-tactical moves on the outermost two lines whose existing orthogonal neighbors are all empty before the move. When choosing the move, subtract δ(m) = 0.12 times the Manhattan distance of m from the center (4,4). Outside this opening, δ(m) = 0.

For how moves are counted, see the note at the end of the preceding section.

Passing and automatic finish

A position where every eligible move is eye-filling is called the “endgame stage”. At this stage, the Level 1 policy chooses pass, and the Level 2 policy also chooses pass if no tactical move is available. The Level 5–7 policies switch to the “endgame-stage move selection” described below. Levels 3–4 follow the policy chosen for that turn.

In Level 1–2 games, an automatic-finish prompt appears upon reaching the endgame stage. Starting automatic finish from the prompt or the Auto button first lets the selected bot level play both sides, excluding eye-filling moves from its candidates during this phase. When either player reaches the endgame stage, or the bot attempts a second consecutive pass or a move that recreates a board position seen during this automatic-finish run, both sides use the endgame-stage move selection for the rest of the run.

Endgame-stage move selection uses the following scoring rule. 25 × captured stones + 1.5 × min(liberties of the resulting friendly group,6) − 25 if that group has one liberty + 12 × adjacent own stones in one-liberty groups before the move + 1000 × number of resolved groups + a uniform random number in [0,2) − 0.12 × Manhattan distance to center

A “resolved group” is either an opposing group that was captured or a group that had one liberty before the move and at least two liberties afterward.

The candidate moves for selection are all eligible moves except the following:

  • Moves that create a friendly group of two or more stones with only one liberty without resolving any group
  • During automatic finish, moves that recreate a board position already seen in that automatic-finish run

If any remaining candidate is not eye-filling, select the highest-scoring move among those candidates. Otherwise, select the highest-scoring move among all remaining candidates. Pass if no candidates remain.

Reference

The evaluation term “own average liberties − opponent average liberties” is based on the Average Liberty Player in Section 6 of Silver, Sutton & Müller, Reinforcement Learning of Local Shape in the Game of Go (IJCAI2007).
